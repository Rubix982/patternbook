### Linocut (`lumenary:linocut`, models this process)

_From: Printmaking; when: Early 1900s onward; works best on: Strong portraits and landscapes with clear forms._

A linocut is printed from a block with the light parts cut away, so tone has to come from area and line: the darkest places are left solid, middle tones become lines that thicken with darkness, and the lights are gouged out. This treatment lays lines across the picture and bends them with the picture’s brightness, so they swing round shapes the way a carver’s hand does.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Lay a set of parallel lines across the picture at the chosen angle.
2. Bend each line’s coordinate with slow noise and with the picture’s brightness.
3. For every pixel measure its distance from the middle of its line and ink it if that is smaller than a width that grows with darkness.
4. Add a slow swell along each line to mimic the taper of a V-shaped gouge.
5. Add solid ink along strong edges as carved outlines, and knock small holes in the ink with roller specks.

**The maths:**

$$u=\frac{n\,(x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta)}{W}+3.2\,w\,(\nu-\tfrac12)+1.5\,w\,(\tfrac12-L_b),\qquad f=u-\lfloor u\rfloor$$

_Line coordinate: n lines across, θ the direction, w the wander slider and ν slow noise; f is the position within a line._ (primitives: tone-line-screen, smooth-noise)

$$q=\min\!\big(1.08,\ 1.12\,d^{0.85}\,s\big),\quad s=0.78+0.45\,\nu',\qquad d=1-\text{contrast}(L_b)$$

_Cut width q from the darkness d, with the taper s along the line._ (primitives: tone-line-screen, tone-curve, smooth-noise)

$$a=\operatorname{smoothstep}\big(\lvert 2f-1\rvert-0.08,\ \lvert 2f-1\rvert+0.02,\ q\big)\ \text{if } q>0.06,\ \text{else }0$$

_Ink coverage a at the pixel._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

**Dials:** `lines` (Cuts across) = 64 [24 to 120]; `angle` (Direction of cuts) = 38 [0 to 180]; `wave` (Wander) = 45 [0 to 100]; `contrast` (Contrast) = 25 [-40 to 80]; `outline` (Carved outlines) = true; `ink` (Ink) = "black" (black, indigo, rust, green).

**History:** Linoleum was patented by Frederick Walton in the 1860s as a floor covering. By the early 1900s printmakers, among them the German expressionists of Die Brücke, were carving it because it was softer than wood. Pablo Picasso made a famous series of colour linocuts in the 1950s, and the technique remains popular in schools and studios.

### Ukiyo-e (`lumenary:ukiyo-e`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Edo, now Tokyo, Japan; when: Seventeenth to nineteenth century; works best on: Landscapes, skies and pictures with clear shapes._

A woodblock print can only lay down flat colour, one block at a time, so the picture has to be reduced to a few solid areas before anything is carved. That limit is the look: a firm black key line, a handful of pigments, and a soft fade of colour called bokashi in the sky, which the printer makes by wiping the block. The slight slip between blocks and the grain of the wood remind you that it was pressed by hand.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Blur the picture by 0.9 times the simplify slider and raise its saturation a little.
2. Match every pixel to the nearest of eight pigments an Edo printer would have had: Prussian blue, vermilion, ochre, sage, pink, grey-brown, paper and sumi ink.
3. Run a majority filter over a 5 by 5 neighbourhood twice to remove specks that a knife could never cut.
4. Draw the key line from edges, add bokashi fades in the sky, and let the wood grain and paper fibre show through the ink.
5. Shift each colour layer slightly to imitate misregistration between blocks, and stamp an artist’s seal.

**The maths:**

$$k(\mathbf x)=\arg\min_j\ d\big(\mathbf c'(\mathbf x),\,\mathbf p_j\big),\qquad s'=1.15\,s+0.04$$

_Each pixel takes the index of the nearest pigment p_j to its blurred colour c′ (saturation s raised to s′), using a redmean-weighted colour distance._ (primitives: palette-snap, saturation-adjust)

$$k'(\mathbf x)=\arg\max_j\ \big|\{\mathbf y\in N(\mathbf x):\ k(\mathbf y)=j\}\big|$$

_The majority filter: the most common index among the neighbours N(x) wins._ (primitives: mode-filter)

$$\lambda=\max\!\Big(e_{\text{idx}}\,\big(0.5+0.5\,w\big),\ w\,\operatorname{smoothstep}\big(\tau,\,2.1\tau,\,\lVert\nabla L\rVert\big)\Big),\qquad \tau=0.24-0.17\,w$$

_The key line: e_idx is 1 on a boundary between colours, ‖∇L‖ the gradient of the lightly blurred brightness and w the key line slider (0 to 1). The ink is mixed in with strength λ, at most 0.95._ (primitives: label-boundary, edge-outline)

$$t_{\text{top}}=b\Big(1-\tfrac{y}{0.38H}\Big)_+^{1.5}\big(0.5+0.2\,n\big),\qquad C\leftarrow(1-t)\,C+t\,C_{\text{blue}}$$

_Bokashi at the top: b is the slider and n a slow noise that makes the edge of the fade uneven._ (primitives: mix-over, smooth-noise)

**Dials:** `simplify` (Simplify) = 5 [1 to 10]; `line` (Key line) = 60 [0 to 100]; `bokashi` (Bokashi fade) = 60 [0 to 100]; `wood` (Wood grain) = 55 [0 to 100]; `reg` (Block misregistration) = 35 [0 to 100]; `paper` (Paper) = "washi" (washi, aged, white); `seal` (Artist seal) = true.

**History:** Ukiyo-e means pictures of the floating world, the pleasure districts and theatres of Edo. Suzuki Harunobu produced the first full colour prints in 1765, and Katsushika Hokusai and Utagawa Hiroshige made landscapes famous in the early nineteenth century, including Hokusai’s Great Wave. Prussian blue, imported in the 1820s, gave the skies and seas of that period their depth. The prints reached Europe in the nineteenth century and influenced artists such as Monet and Van Gogh.

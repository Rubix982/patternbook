### Blueprint (`lumenary:blueprint`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Architecture and engineering offices; when: 1840s process, widely used 1870s to 1990s; works best on: Buildings, machines and objects with clear outlines._

A blueprint is a drawing meant to be read, so it is made of lines: outlines, hatching, dimensions and a title block. This treatment turns your picture into exactly that. Edges become drawn lines, shadow becomes hatching, and the page is furnished with a drafting grid and annotations, so the photograph reads like a technical study.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Blur the picture and find its edges, with the line weight setting how coarse the neighbourhood is.
2. Where the picture is darker than a threshold, draw a field of diagonal lines, and where it is darker still, cross it with a second set.
3. Add a drafting grid, with every fifth line stronger.
4. Mix paper and line colours according to the strength of lines, hatching and grid.
5. Draw the double border, dimension lines with arrowheads, the title block and registration crosses.

**The maths:**

$$a=\max\Big(S_{\theta,1.7\theta}(\lvert\nabla L_b\rvert),\ a_{\text{hatch}},\ a_{\text{grid}}\Big)$$

_Line strength at a pixel is the strongest of edge, hatching and grid._ (primitives: edge-outline)

$$a_{\text{hatch}}=\begin{cases}0.5&D>\lambda_1\ \text{and}\ (x+y)\bmod s<w\\0.4&D>\lambda_2+0.2\ \text{and}\ (x-y)\bmod s<w\end{cases},\qquad D=1-\bar L$$

_Cross-hatching: D is local darkness; the modulo picks out evenly spaced diagonals._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$C=C_{\text{paper}}+(C_{\text{line}}-C_{\text{paper}})\,a$$

_Linear mix of paper and ink._ (primitives: mix-over)

**Dials:** `scheme` (Sheet) = "blueprint" (blueprint, whiteprint, mono); `detail` (Line detail) = 55 [0 to 100]; `weight` (Line weight) = 1 [1 to 3]; `hatch` (Hatching) = 45 [0 to 100]; `grid` (Grid) = true; `notes` (Annotations) = true; `title` (Title) = "STUDY NO. 18".

**History:** The cyanotype process, invented by Sir John Herschel in 1842, made white lines on a deep blue ground and let engineers copy drawings cheaply. Anna Atkins used it in the 1840s for her botanical books. Architects and engineers relied on blueprints from the 1870s until diazo prints and then computer drawing replaced them, and the name stuck for any detailed plan.

### Cyanotype (`lumenary:cyanotype`, models this process)

_From: Early photography; when: 1842 onward; works best on: Silhouettes, plants and pictures with strong light and shade._

A cyanotype is a sun print: paper coated with iron salts turns Prussian blue where light strikes it and washes white where it does not. It has no greys other than varying blue. This treatment maps the picture’s brightness onto that chemistry and adds what hand coating always brings: blotches, brush strokes and a ragged edge where the liquid pooled.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Stretch the picture, shift it by the exposure slider and add contrast.
2. Send the result through a five colour ramp from deep blue in the shadows to near white in the highlights. Tea and green toning swap in other ramps.
3. Change the tone slightly with two noises, one slow and blotchy and one long and thin like a brush stroke.
4. Stop the coating short of the sheet edge in a ragged line from two more noises, with a darker pooled edge.
5. Show bare cream paper outside the edge and multiply a fine fibre noise over everything.

**The maths:**

$$t=\operatorname{clamp}\big(\text{contrast}\big(L+0.4\,\epsilon+\pi,\ c\big)\big),\qquad \pi=0.28\,u\,(n_1-\tfrac12)+0.12\,u\,(n_2-\tfrac12)$$

_Tone t: ε is the exposure slider, c the contrast and u the uneven-coating slider._ (primitives: tone-curve, smooth-noise)

$$C=\text{ramp}(t),\qquad c=\operatorname{smoothstep}(-2\sigma,\ 2\sigma,\ e-m+\rho)$$

_Colour from the five-stop ramp; c is how coated the pixel is: e the distance to the sheet edge, m = 0.055 of the short side, ρ the ragged-edge noise, σ the picture scale._ (primitives: colour-ramp, soft-threshold, sheet-edge)

$$C\leftarrow C\,\big(1-0.8\,p\big),\qquad p=0.22\,c\,\big(1-\operatorname{smoothstep}(0,\ 9\sigma,\ e-m+\rho)\big)$$

_Darkening where the liquid pooled along the edge._ (primitives: multiply-blend, soft-threshold, sheet-edge)

**Dials:** `tone` (Chemistry) = "classic" (classic, tea, green); `exposure` (Exposure) = 0 [-40 to 40]; `contrast` (Contrast) = 20 [-30 to 80]; `uneven` (Uneven coating) = 45 [0 to 100]; `brush` (Brushed edge) = true; `seed` (Recoat) = 1.

**History:** Sir John Herschel invented the cyanotype in 1842. In 1843 Anna Atkins used it to publish photographic records of seaweeds, often called the first book illustrated with photographs. Engineers later copied their drawings with it, producing the original blueprints, and artists and schoolchildren still make sun prints with the same chemistry.

### Art deco (`lumenary:art-deco`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Paris, then New York and Bombay; when: 1920s to 1940s; works best on: Portraits and buildings with clear light and dark shapes._

Art deco loved machines, speed and rich materials, so it drew with metal: strict symmetry, stepped outlines and lines that swell and thin like those on a banknote. Here brightness sets the thickness of each gold line, so the light parts of your picture become fat, gleaming metal and the dark parts shrink to hairlines. The picture is carried by line weight alone, which is exactly how an engraver works.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Cover the page with fine horizontal bands, or with rays fanning out from a point below the picture in Sunburst mode.
2. Measure how bright the picture is along each band or ray.
3. Make the line thicker where it is bright and thinner where it is dark.
4. Fill every line with one metallic gradient laid across the whole page, so it reads as polished gold, silver or copper.
5. Add the stepped double frame, the corner fans and the title.

**The maths:**

$$w(x)=\text{step}\,\big(0.05+0.9\,L(x,y_c)^{0.9}\big)$$

_Thickness of a line band at x; y_c is the band’s centre line._ (primitives: tone-line-screen, tone-curve)

$$\hat{\mathbf d}_k=(\sin\alpha_k,\,-\cos\alpha_k),\quad\alpha_k=-\beta+\big(k+\tfrac12\big)\Delta\alpha,\quad\beta=82^{\circ}$$

_Direction of ray k._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$w(r)=\min\!\Big(0.96\,\Delta\alpha\,r,\ \ \Delta\alpha\,r\,\big(0.05+0.9\,L^{0.9}\big)\Big)$$

_Ray width at distance r from the origin: never wider than its slice Δα·r._ (primitives: tone-line-screen, tone-curve)

**Dials:** `mode` (Engraving) = "lines" (lines, rays); `density` (Line count) = 84 [30 to 160]; `contrast` (Contrast) = 20 [-50 to 60]; `metal` (Metal) = "gold" (gold, silver, copper, white); `frame` (Frame) = true; `title` (Title) = "N° 19".

**History:** The style takes its name from the 1925 Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes in Paris. In the 1920s and 30s it spread through posters, cinemas, ocean liners and skyscrapers, and its stepped silhouettes and sunbursts still mark cities from New York to Mumbai. The thick and thin engraved lines used for the metal come from banknote and certificate printing, where fine line work was prized because it was hard to copy.

### Victorian (`lumenary:victorian`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: British and American printing houses; when: 1830s to 1900; works best on: Portraits, with a clear face against a plain background._

An engraver cannot paint grey. Tone has to come from lines: thick and close together for shadow, thin and far apart for light. This treatment does the same with horizontal lines that swell and shrink with the darkness beneath them, adds crossing lines in the deepest shadows, and sets the result in an oval with an ornate frame, as keepsake portraits were.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Draw many horizontal lines whose thickness follows the darkness of the picture beneath.
2. Make each line wander gently up and down on a sine wave, for the hand-cut look.
3. Add a second set of diagonal lines in the darkest places, lifting the pen where the picture is lighter than a threshold.
4. Clip the portrait to an oval and frame it with two rules and a ring of 72 beads.
5. Add the double border, corner rosettes and a caption between two rules.

**The maths:**

$$t(x,i)=1.05\,\big(1-L(x,y_i)\big)\,\Delta y$$

_Thickness of line i at column x; Δy is the line spacing._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$y(x,i)=y_i+0.18\,\Delta y\,\sin(0.045\,x+0.7\,i)$$

_The gentle waver of each line._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$\text{hatch where}\quad L<0.4\cdot\text{cross}+0.05$$

_Cross-hatching is only drawn on dark places._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

**Dials:** `ink` (Ink) = "sepia" (sepia, black, blue); `lines` (Engraved lines) = 110 [50 to 180]; `cross` (Cross-hatching) = 50 [0 to 100]; `oval` (Oval cameo) = true; `caption` (Caption) = "A VIEW OF THE ROOM".

**History:** Steel replaced copper for engraving plates in the 1820s because it lasted for far longer print runs. Steel-engraved portraits, book illustrations and banknotes were everywhere in the Victorian era (1837 to 1901), before photography and halftone printing took over. The wandering line and cross-hatching used here are the engraver’s tools for building tone from nothing but black ink.

### Banknote Engraving (`lumenary:banknote-engraving`, models this process)

_From: Security printing; when: 19th century onward; works best on: Portraits with clear faces._

Banknotes were made to be difficult to forge, so their portraits are built from fine engraved lines whose thickness changes with tone, surrounded by guilloche rosettes drawn by rolling one circle inside another. This treatment does both: thick ribbons in the shadows, hairlines in the lights, and curves that look like machined lacework.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Draw the picture as horizontal ribbons inside a rounded window.
2. Walk along each line in short steps, read the darkness beneath, and set the half thickness to a power of that darkness, capped at half the line spacing.
3. Shift lines down where the picture is light and up where it is dark, and add a gentle sine wave so the surface looks modelled.
4. Add the security devices: a faint tint of fine wavering lines, a double frame, and guilloche rosettes repeated seven times with slightly different pen offsets.
5. Set an invented numeral, serial number and title in a Bodoni face.

**The maths:**

$$\text{half}=\min\!\Big(\tfrac p2,\ 0.52\,p\,d^{0.9}+0.18\,\sigma\Big),\qquad d=1-\text{contrast}(L,0.2)$$

_Half thickness of the ribbon; p is the line spacing, σ the scale of the picture._ (primitives: tone-line-screen, tone-curve)

$$y_c=y_i+(\tfrac12-d)\,1.4\,p\,\kappa+0.35\,p\,\kappa\,\sin(0.018\,x+0.45\,i)$$

_Centre line of ribbon i, κ being the swing slider._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$\begin{aligned}x&=(R-r)\cos t+\delta\cos\!\Big(\tfrac{R-r}{r}\,t\Big)\\ y&=(R-r)\sin t-\delta\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{R-r}{r}\,t\Big)\end{aligned},\quad r=0.34R,\ \delta\approx0.78R$$

_The guilloche curve (hypotrochoid), traced over eight turns of t._ (primitives: parametric-curve)

**Dials:** `title` (Title) = "NOTE OF LIGHT"; `value` (Value) = "100"; `lines` (Engraved lines) = 84 [40 to 140]; `swing` (Line swing) = 50 [0 to 100]; `ink` (Ink) = "green" (green, blue, brown, violet); `rosettes` (Rosettes) = true.

**History:** Engraved banknotes grew from the engraving of plates for books and bank bills, and the early nineteenth century saw intricate machine-turned patterns added against forgery. The first postage stamp, the Penny Black of 1840, had an engine-turned background for the same reason. Today’s banknotes still combine engraving and guilloche with newer security features. The wording in this treatment is invented, and the note is not real money.

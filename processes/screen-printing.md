### Retro (`lumenary:retro`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Seventies print and packaging; when: 1970s; works best on: Warm outdoor scenes and portraits._

Seventies colour was warm and earthy: harvest gold, burnt orange, avocado and brown. Limited inks also meant flat colour layers, so this treatment cuts the picture into a few bands, one ink each, and lets the edges between them wobble the way imperfectly aligned screen prints do. The cream border and stripes finish the sense of a printed object.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Pick the palette and thin it to the number of bands you asked for.
2. Add a tiny ordered offset to each pixel so that band edges come out slightly ragged.
3. Cut the brightness, with adjustable contrast, into bands and print one colour per band.
4. Draw the three swooping stripes and add film grain.
5. Cut a rounded hole from a cream rectangle for the border.

**The maths:**

$$d(x,y)=0.045\left(\frac{4\,(x\ \&\ 3)+(y\ \&\ 3)}{16}-\tfrac12\right)$$

_Ordered offset from a 4 × 4 pattern; & 3 keeps the low two bits._ (primitives: ordered-dither)

$$\text{band}=\operatorname{clamp}\big(\lfloor (c(L)+d)\,n\rfloor,\ 0,\ n-1\big)$$

_c(L) is the brightness after the contrast slider._ (primitives: tone-bands, tone-curve)

**Dials:** `palette` (Palette) = "sunset" (sunset, avocado, denim); `bands` (Colour bands) = 5 [3 to 6]; `contrast` (Contrast) = 20 [-50 to 80]; `swoosh` (Stripes) = true; `frame` (Cream frame) = true; `grain` (Print grain) = 40 [0 to 100].

**History:** Screen printing became a major commercial process in the first half of the twentieth century and was taken up by artists such as Andy Warhol in the 1960s. In the 1970s it filled posters, record sleeves and packaging with earth-toned flat colour, and that palette is now shorthand for the decade.

### Pop Art (`lumenary:pop-art`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Britain and the United States; when: 1950s and 1960s; works best on: Faces and strong silhouettes._

Pop art borrowed the look of mass printing: flat inks, bold outlines and the dots that cheap presses used to fake shading. Dots that grow as the tone gets darker let a few flat inks suggest smooth shading, and the 2 × 2 grid repeats the picture in different colours, the way a screen printer pulls the same image in several editions.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Divide brightness into a few bands and print each in one flat ink.
2. Near the boundary with the next band, show the next ink as dots that grow as the pixel approaches it.
3. Place the dots on a staggered grid, every second column shifted by half a cell.
4. Draw outlines from edge strength in the darkest ink.
5. In grid layout, repeat the picture four times with a different set of inks in each panel.

**The maths:**

$$u=n\,c(L),\qquad b=\min(n-1,\lfloor u\rfloor),\qquad f=u-b$$

_Band b and the position f (0 to 1) inside it._ (primitives: tone-bands, tone-curve)

$$\text{dot}:\ \ (x_c)^2+(y_c)^2<\big(0.72\,s\,f\big)^2$$

_A pixel is inside a dot of the next ink when its distance from the cell centre is less than 0.72·s·f._ (primitives: tone-dot-lattice)

$$\lvert\nabla L\rvert>0.35-0.28\cdot\text{outline}$$

_Where the outline ink replaces everything else._ (primitives: edge-outline)

**Dials:** `layout` (Panels) = "grid" (grid, single); `levels` (Inks per panel) = 4 [3 to 4]; `dots` (Ben-Day dots) = 60 [0 to 100]; `dotSize` (Dot size) = 9 [4 to 20]; `outline` (Outline) = 55 [0 to 100]; `contrast` (Contrast) = 30 [-30 to 80].

**History:** Pop art began in Britain in the 1950s, for example in Richard Hamilton’s 1956 collage about the modern home, and flourished in the United States in the 1960s. Roy Lichtenstein enlarged the dots of comic printing into paintings, and Andy Warhol repeated screen-printed celebrity faces in grids. The dot method is named after Benjamin Day, a nineteenth century printer.

### Screen Print (`lumenary:screen-print`, models this process)

_From: Stencil printing in Asia, then commercial presses and art studios; when: Commercial use from the 1900s, pop art in the 1960s; works best on: Bold portraits and graphic scenes._

Screen printing pushes ink through a mesh, one colour at a time. Cut the picture into brightness bands, print the lightest ink over the darkest regions and add darker inks over smaller areas, and the dark parts receive every ink in turn. Because inks are transparent they multiply, so overlaps create new colours without extra plates.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Cut the picture into bands: the lightest ink covers every pixel darker than 0.84, the next everything darker than 0.62, then 0.41, then 0.21.
2. Print each ink as a separate flat layer.
3. Combine the layers by multiplication, so yellow over red becomes orange and the full stack is near black.
4. Shift each pass by its own small random offset for misregistration.
5. Wobble the thresholds with smooth noise for ragged edges and add a faint cross-hatch from the mesh.

**The maths:**

$$\text{cov}_k=1-\operatorname{smoothstep}\big(\vartheta_k-0.012,\ \vartheta_k+0.012,\ L(\mathbf x+\mathbf o_k)+r\big),\qquad \vartheta=(0.84,\,0.62,\,0.41,\,0.21)$$

_Coverage by ink k: o_k is that pass’s offset and r the edge noise._ (primitives: tone-bands, misregistration, soft-threshold)

$$C=C_{\text{paper}}\prod_k\Big(1-\text{cov}_k\,\phi_k\,\big(1-I_k/255\big)\Big),\qquad \phi_k=0.93+0.07\,n$$

_Multiply overprint; φ is the unevenness of the ink film._ (primitives: multiply-blend)

$$w=1-0.07\,m\,\Big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos\tfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\cos\tfrac{2\pi y}{\lambda}\Big)$$

_Mesh texture: m is the slider and λ the mesh spacing._ (primitives: material-ground)

**Dials:** `palette` (Inks) = "poster" (poster, sunset, forest, ice); `passes` (Number of inks) = 4 [2 to 4]; `miss` (Misregistration) = 35 [0 to 100]; `rough` (Rough edges) = 40 [0 to 100]; `mesh` (Mesh texture) = 40 [0 to 100]; `seed` (Pull again) = 1.

**History:** Stencil printing through silk or fine mesh was used in East Asia for centuries. Commercial screen printing spread in the early 1900s, and in the 1930s artists proposed the name serigraphy for fine art prints. Andy Warhol made the technique famous in the 1960s, and it is still the main way to print posters, t-shirts and gig bills.

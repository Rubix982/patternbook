### Swiss Design (`lumenary:swiss-design`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Zurich and Basel; when: 1940s to 1960s; works best on: Strong, simple compositions with a clear tonal range._

Swiss designers believed that a clear grid and honest photography could communicate more than decoration. So the page here is built on a six column grid, the picture is rendered as a plain line screen of black bars, and a single red shape and heavy sans-serif title do all the rest. Everything lines up, and nothing is there by accident.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Set a six column grid and place the picture across columns two to six.
2. Draw the picture as horizontal rows of thin vertical bars, whose height is the local darkness.
3. Let bright areas have no bars and dark areas fill the row.
4. Place one red shape across the picture’s edge.
5. Set the title flush left at the size that spans the picture, with small text, a rule and a page number on the same grid.

**The maths:**

$$h(x,y_i)=0.98\,\big(1-L(x,y_i)\big)\,\Delta y,\qquad \Delta y=\frac{h_{\text{img}}}{N_{\text{lines}}}$$

_Height of the bar at column x on line i; the lines are Δy apart._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$s=\min\!\Big(24u,\ \frac{0.98\,(w_{\text{img}}+c)}{w_{100}}\cdot100\Big)$$

_Title size: c is the column width and w₁₀₀ the title’s measured width at size 100._ (written for this model: layout)

**Dials:** `title` (Title) = "Form"; `sub` (Subtitle) = "Image study no. 1"; `lines` (Line density) = 90 [40 to 160]; `shape` (Red shape) = "circle" (circle, square, none); `accent` (Red) = "#e4261f"; `invert` (Black ground) = false.

**History:** The International Typographic Style grew in Switzerland in the 1940s and 50s through designers such as Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann and Emil Ruder. The typeface Helvetica appeared in 1957, and the journal Neue Grafik spread the ideas worldwide. Its principles, grid, sans-serif type and objective photography, still underpin much of modern graphic design.

### Comic book (`lumenary:comic-book`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: American comic printing; when: 1930s onward; works best on: Faces and action scenes with clear edges._

Cheap comics were printed with a few flat inks, black outlines and dots, so the inked look is mostly a matter of constraints. Posterising limits the colours, edge detection supplies the pen lines, and a lattice of dots in the shadows imitates the press. Together they turn photographic realism into drawing.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Snap brightness to a small number of levels and rescale each pixel’s colour so it keeps its hue at the flat level.
2. Raise saturation around each pixel’s own grey value.
3. Blur the picture, find edges, and darken pixels toward black in proportion to edge strength.
4. In darker but not black tones, lay a lattice of dots on a 45 degree grid.
5. Darken any pixel that falls inside a dot whose radius grows as the tone gets darker.

**The maths:**

$$q=\frac{\operatorname{round}\big(L\,(n-1)\big)}{n-1},\qquad C'=C\cdot\frac{q+0.06}{L+0.06}$$

_Posterise to n levels and rescale colour to the new brightness; the 0.06 keeps blacks stable._ (primitives: tone-bands)

$$u=\frac{x+y}{\sqrt2\,s},\quad v=\frac{y-x}{\sqrt2\,s},\qquad\text{dot if }\sqrt{(u-\lfloor u\rceil)^2+(v-\lfloor v\rceil)^2}<0.18+0.32\Big(1-\frac{q}{0.62}\Big)$$

_Dot lattice at 45° with spacing s; ⌊·⌉ rounds to the nearest integer. Applied for tones 0.02 < q < 0.62; dots darken colour to 50%._ (primitives: tone-dot-lattice)

$$C_{\text{out}}=C\,\big(1-S_{\theta,1.35\theta}(\lvert\nabla L_b\rvert)\big)$$

_Ink: edge strength darkens toward black._ (primitives: edge-outline)

**Dials:** `levels` (Color steps) = 4 [2 to 8]; `pop` (Color pop) = 55 [0 to 100]; `ink` (Ink lines) = 55 [0 to 100]; `thick` (Line weight) = 2 [1 to 4]; `dots` (Print dots) = true; `dotSize` (Dot size) = 90 [40 to 160].

**History:** Comic books took their classic form in the late 1930s, when Superman appeared in Action Comics in 1938. Their four-colour printing could not make smooth gradients, so artists and printers used flat colour and dot patterns instead. Pop artists such as Roy Lichtenstein later enlarged those dots into fine art in the early 1960s.

### Newspaper (`lumenary:newspaper`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Printing presses and newsrooms; when: Halftone newspapers since the 1880s; works best on: Strong portraits and news-like scenes._

Newspapers cannot print grey. They print black dots of different sizes on pale paper and rely on your eye to blend them, so a photograph is really a field of dots. This treatment lays a lattice of such dots over the picture and builds the rest of a front page around it with a masthead, a headline, rules and columns.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Lay a lattice of dots rotated by 45 degrees, the angle where a regular pattern is least noticeable.
2. At each lattice point read the brightness and draw a dot whose radius grows with the square root of the darkness, so inked area follows darkness.
3. Fit the masthead to the page width by measuring and scaling it.
4. Wrap the headline into at most three lines, shrinking it by 8 percent steps until it fits.
5. Fill the columns with greeked text, bars standing in for words, and add paper grain and a warm vignette.

**The maths:**

$$\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}a+b\\ b-a\end{pmatrix},\qquad a,b\in s\,\mathbb Z$$

_Lattice points: a square grid of spacing s turned by 45°._ (primitives: tone-dot-lattice)

$$r=0.62\,s\,\sqrt{1-L(x,y)}$$

_Dot radius; the inked fraction of a cell is πr²/s², proportional to the darkness 1 − L._ (primitives: tone-dot-lattice)

$$F=\min\!\left(u_0,\ \frac{0.98\,w_{\text{page}}}{w_{100}}\cdot 100\right)$$

_Masthead font size: the text measured at 100 px has width w₁₀₀, and is scaled to fill 98% of the page width._ (written for this model: layout)

**Dials:** `masthead` (Masthead) = "The Daily Lumenary"; `headline` (Headline) = "Picture Made Entirely Of Numbers, Experts Confirm"; `dot` (Dot size) = 6 [3 to 12]; `paper` (Paper) = "newsprint" (newsprint, grey, white); `columns` (Columns) = 4 [3 to 5]; `seed` (Re-set type) = 1.

**History:** The first halftone photograph in a daily newspaper appeared in the New York Daily Graphic in 1880, made by Stephen Horgan. Frederic Ives patented a halftone process in 1881, and crossed-line screens soon made it routine. By the early twentieth century newspapers printed photographs every day, and the dot became so familiar that artists later made it into pop art.

### Halftone (`lumenary:halftone`, models this process)

_From: Photoengraving and offset printing; when: 1880s onward; works best on: Portraits and high contrast photographs._

A press can lay down ink or leave paper bare, nothing in between, so printers fake grey with dots: small for light, large for dark. Your eye blends them when they are small enough. Colour printing repeats that with four inks, each on its own lattice turned to a different angle, so the dots interleave in a neat rosette instead of fighting each other.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Rotate a square lattice by the screen angle and lay it over the picture.
2. Sample the picture at each lattice point.
3. Draw a circle whose area is proportional to the ink that tone needs, so the radius is the square root of the tone.
4. In four colour mode, separate the picture into cyan, magenta, yellow and black and draw four lattices at 15, 75, 0 and 45 degrees with the multiply blend.
5. In colour on black mode, let dots grow with brightness and take the cell’s colour.

**The maths:**

$$\mathbf p_{ij}=\mathbf c+s\,R(\theta)\begin{bmatrix}i\\ j\end{bmatrix},\qquad R(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$$

_Dot centres on a lattice of spacing s rotated by θ about the picture centre c._ (primitives: tone-dot-lattice)

$$r=\tfrac12\,s\,g\cdot1.06\,\sqrt{\operatorname{adj}(1-L)}\qquad\text{since}\quad\pi r^{2}\propto\text{ink}$$

_Radius of a one-ink dot; g is dot gain and adj() the contrast curve._ (primitives: tone-dot-lattice, tone-curve)

$$K=1-\max(R,G,B),\qquad C=\frac{1-R-K}{1-K},\quad M=\frac{1-G-K}{1-K},\quad Y=\frac{1-B-K}{1-K}$$

_RGB to CMYK separation (channels in 0 to 1)._ (written for this model: specific)

**Dials:** `cells` (Dots across) = 80 [30 to 170]; `mode` (Inks) = "mono" (mono, cmyk, color); `angle` (Screen angle) = 45 [0 to 90]; `contrast` (Contrast) = 10 [-50 to 50]; `gain` (Dot gain) = 1 [0.7 to 1.3]; `paper` (Paper) = "#efede6"; `ink` (Ink (one ink)) = "#0c0c0c".

**History:** Stephen Horgan printed the first halftone photograph in a daily newspaper in 1880, and Frederic Ives patented a halftone process in 1881. Four colour offset printing through the twentieth century made the dot the basis of nearly every magazine, and it is why a close look at a printed photograph shows rosettes.

## patternbook's model: `halftone`

_The theory of patternbook's own `halftone` treatment (`treatments/treatments.js`), "Halftone", moved here from `treatments/theory/halftone.md` on 2026-11-22 so it sits with the process it models._

**For:** photographs on a page in a one- or two-ink print style.
**Comes from:** photoengraving and newspaper printing, from the 1880s on.
**Works best on:** portraits and photos with clear light and shadow.

### Why it looks this way

A press can't print grey, so a photograph is turned into dots on a regular lattice: small dots
where it's light, large where it's dark. Far enough away, the eye blends them back into tone.
The lattice is turned to an angle (45° is traditional for one ink), because rows of dots at 0°
are easy for the eye to pick out. The same press imperfections apply: dots spread, edges
wander, and solids starve a little.

### How it works

1. Brightness L for each pixel, through the contrast curve; ink needed c = 1 − L.
2. Lay a lattice of spacing s, rotated by the screen angle, over the picture.
3. For each lattice cell, read c at the cell's centre, so the whole dot has one size.
4. Draw the dot with area proportional to c, slightly spread, with wobbly edges and grain.

### The maths

A dot of area c·s² in a cell of area s² has radius

    r = s · √(c / π)

and a pixel at distance d from the dot centre is inked by
smoothstep(r + 0.7 px + g, r − 0.7 px + g, d), where g is per-pixel grain. The dot edge wanders
by value noise of amplitude `wobble`. Spread raises c by up to 35% of `spread`. At c = 1 the
radius 0.56 s leaves the cell corners bare, as a real screen does before dots merge.

Dot centres in picture coordinates come from rounding the rotated coordinates
(u, v) = R(−θ)(x, y)/s and rotating back.

### Dials

`screen` (5 px dot spacing), `angle` (45°), `contrast` (0.15), `spread`, `wobble`, `grain`,
`starve`, `fibre`, `ink`, `paper`.

### When it fights the content

- **Small photos.** Below about 20 dots across, the subject disappears.
- **Line art.** Screening a drawing turns every line into a row of dots; use `press`.

### Azulejo (`lumenary:azulejo`, models this process)

_From: Portugal and Spain; when: Sixteenth century onward; works best on: Pictures with large soft areas._

An azulejo painter works with cobalt oxide on an unfired white glaze of tin. The cobalt sinks in as the tile is fired and cannot be corrected, so the thickness of the paint decides how dark the blue becomes. Tiles are painted one by one so that the corner of each tile holds part of a rosette, and four tiles together make a whole flower.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Blur the picture twice and map it to a four-stop ramp from deep cobalt through mid blue and a pale wash to white glaze, with a streak of noise standing for the brush.
2. Lay tiles on a grid with a thin grout line and a small bevel lit from the top left.
3. Paint a pattern on every tile so that the corner of one tile holds a quarter of a rosette, completed by its three neighbours.
4. Add brush lines and glaze crackle.
5. Choose a pigment: cobalt, manganese purple or copper green.

**The maths:**

$$a=\min(u,1-u),\quad b=\min(v,1-v),\quad \rho=\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\quad \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$$

_Distances from the nearest tile corner, in tile units (u and v run 0 to 1 across the tile)._ (primitives: lattice-repeat)

$$\text{petal if } \rho<0.3\,\big(0.56+0.44\,|\cos 4\theta|\big),\qquad \text{ring if } |\rho-0.4|<0.014$$

_The quarter rosette. |cos 4θ| has eight lobes round a full circle, so four tiles meeting make eight petals._ (primitives: star-rosette-motif)

$$C\leftarrow C+q\,(P-C),\qquad q=0.15+0.8\,p,\quad P=\begin{cases}\text{cobalt}&\text{wash}>\tfrac12\\ \text{glaze}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

_The painted pattern: p is the slider, and P the pigment chosen so it always contrasts with the wash beneath._ (primitives: mix-over)

**Dials:** `size` (Tile size) = 80 [40 to 160]; `pattern` (Painted pattern) = 65 [0 to 100]; `line` (Brush lines) = 45 [0 to 100]; `crackle` (Glaze crackle) = 40 [0 to 100]; `ink` (Pigment) = "cobalt" (cobalt, manganese, copper).

**History:** The word comes from the Arabic az-zulayj, small polished stone. Portugal and Spain took up painted tiles in the sixteenth century, and the Portuguese king Manuel I is said to have admired tiles in Seville in 1503. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries blue and white came to cover churches, palaces, stations and the fronts of houses, including the panels at São Bento station in Porto, painted by Jorge Colaço and completed in 1930.

### Delft Blue (`lumenary:delft-blue`, models this process)

_From: Delft, the Netherlands; when: Seventeenth century onward; works best on: Landscapes, buildings and still lifes._

Delftware was the Dutch answer to the Chinese porcelain that ships brought to Europe. It is ordinary earthenware dressed up with white tin glaze and painted in cobalt blue. The painter’s brush follows the form of the thing, and darker passages are built up by going over them more than once, so blue builds into shadow in layers.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Blur the picture heavily to a smooth brightness field F.
2. At every point, find the direction along which F does not change, perpendicular to its gradient, and lay strokes along those lines so they curve round shapes.
3. Scatter seeds on a jittered grid and shuffle them.
4. In each layer, paint at a seed only if the brightness there is below a threshold that starts at 0.70 and falls by 0.17 for each layer, so dark places are painted over several times.
5. Add outlines, glaze crackle and, if chosen, a plate shape with a rim.

**The maths:**

$$\mathbf t=\frac{\big(-\partial_y F,\ \partial_x F\big)}{\lVert\nabla F\rVert},\qquad \mathbf x_{n+1}=\mathbf x_n+\Delta\,\mathbf t(\mathbf x_n)$$

_A stroke follows the isophote, the line of equal brightness. Δ is 2.6 px and the sign of t is chosen to agree with the previous step. Where the gradient is nearly zero a fixed angle of about 0.7 radians takes over._ (primitives: edge-tangent-field, streamline-trace)

$$\text{paint if } T(\mathbf x_0)\le\theta_\ell+0.08\,(\xi-\tfrac12),\qquad \theta_\ell=0.70-0.17\,\ell$$

_Layer ℓ threshold, tested on T, a lightly blurred brightness (the heavy blur F only steers the strokes). ξ is a random number per seed, so the limit is a little ragged._ (primitives: importance-sampling, seeded-random)

$$\alpha=0.2+0.5\,\frac{\theta_\ell-T}{\theta_\ell},\qquad w=w_0\,\big(0.7+0.5\,(1-T)\big)$$

_The darker the place, the more opaque and the wider the stroke._ (primitives: tone-mark-size)

**Dials:** `stroke` (Brush size) = 2.5 [1 to 6]; `length` (Stroke length) = 26 [6 to 60]; `depth` (Layers of paint) = 3 [1 to 4]; `outline` (Outline) = 50 [0 to 100]; `crackle` (Glaze crackle) = 35 [0 to 100]; `shape` (Piece) = "plate" (plate, full); `seed` (New strokes) = 3.

**History:** Dutch potters in Delft developed blue and white earthenware in the seventeenth century, in imitation of Ming porcelain imported by the Dutch East India Company. De Koninklijke Porceleyne Fles, founded in 1653, is still producing Delftware today. Windmills, tulips and landscapes remain popular subjects.

### Talavera (`lumenary:talavera`, models this process)

_From: Puebla, Mexico, descended from Spanish majolica; when: Sixteenth century onward; works best on: Colourful pictures with warm and cool areas._

Talavera is painted by hand, so no two tiles are quite alike: the motif sits a little off-centre, a little tilted, and the line wavers. Its palette of cobalt, yellow, green and orange is limited by what survives the kiln. Each tile here takes its ground from the light in the picture, from cobalt in the shadows to white glaze in the lights.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Make a tile for every cell of a grid and choose its ground by brightness, from cobalt in the shadows through green, orange and yellow to white glaze.
2. Paint two accent colours on each ground, chosen to contrast: yellow and white on cobalt, for example, and cobalt with orange on white.
3. Let the warmth of the picture at that tile (red above blue) decide which of the two accents leads.
4. Paint a four-way flower or star, offset and tilted slightly by a hand wobble.
5. Add grout lines and glaze shine.

**The maths:**

$$g=\min\big(4,\ \lfloor 5\,t\rfloor\big)\ \in\ \{\text{cobalt, green, orange, yellow, glaze}\}$$

_The ground of a tile from its blurred brightness t._ (primitives: tone-bands)

$$\mathbf v_k=r_k\,\Big(\cos\tfrac{k\pi}{8},\ \sin\tfrac{k\pi}{8}\Big),\qquad r_k=\begin{cases}R_1&k\ \text{even}\\ R_2&k\ \text{odd}\end{cases}$$

_The vertices of an eight-pointed star: tips at R₁ and notches at R₂ (0.85 u and 0.42 u for the large star, 0.5 u and 0.26 u for the small one)._ (primitives: star-rosette-motif)

$$\text{lead colour}=\begin{cases}\text{fg}_1&R>B\\ \text{fg}_2&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

_The warm-or-cool rule: R and B are the red and blue values of the picture’s boosted colour at the tile._ (written for this model: specific)

**Dials:** `size` (Tile size) = 62 [34 to 130]; `motif` (Motif) = "mixed" (mixed, flower, star); `hand` (Hand-painted wobble) = 50 [0 to 100]; `grout` (Grout) = 50 [0 to 100]; `seed` (New painting) = 5.

**History:** Talavera de Puebla descends from Spanish majolica, brought to New Spain in the sixteenth century, and it also took on Indigenous and Asian influences. It has a protected designation of origin. In 2019 UNESCO added the artisanal Talavera of Puebla and Tlaxcala, together with the ceramics of Talavera de la Reina and El Puente del Arzobispo in Spain, to its list of intangible heritage.

### Hala Blue Pottery (`lumenary:hala-blue-pottery`, models this process)

_From: Hala, Sindh; when: Centuries old; works best on: Landscapes and pictures with soft shadows._

Blue pottery is painted on white glaze with cobalt and turquoise, then fired once, so the colour sinks into the glaze and cannot be changed. The painter has to know what each stroke will become. The designs are vines, leaves and small flowers, drawn so that every curve leads into the next. Here the picture becomes a cobalt wash, and vines follow its contours across the glaze.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Blur the picture twice (radius smooth × 0.9 px) to a field T.
2. Blend the glaze colour toward cobalt by v = 0.78 w (1 − T)^1.5, where w is the wash slider, so shadows turn deep blue and highlights stay white.
3. Find the contour lines of T at evenly spaced levels by marching squares, join them into curves, and draw them with a width that falls from 3.9 px at the darkest contour to 1.7 px at the lightest.
4. Place leaves on alternate sides of each vine about every 24 px, turned 0.95 radians from the tangent, in turquoise and cobalt by turns.
5. Add small flowers, and the fine crackle of old glaze.

**The maths:**

$$C=C_{\text{glaze}}+\big(C_{\text{cobalt}}-C_{\text{glaze}}\big)\,v,\qquad v=0.78\,w\,(1-T)^{1.5}$$

_Cobalt wash. T is the doubly blurred brightness and w the wash slider (0 to 1)._ (primitives: mix-over, tone-curve)

$$\{\mathbf x:\ T(\mathbf x)=\lambda_k\},\quad \lambda_k=\frac{k}{N},\ \ k=1,\dots,N-1,\qquad w_k=(1.7+2.2\,(1-\lambda_k))\,\mathrm{sc}$$

_Vine k is a contour of T at level λₖ. N is the “vines” slider; darker contours are drawn thicker._ (primitives: iso-contours)

$$e=F_2-F_1<1.1\,\mathrm{sc}\ \Rightarrow\ C\leftarrow C\,\big(1-0.3\,c\,(1-\tfrac{e}{1.1\,\mathrm{sc}})\big)$$

_Crackle: F₁ and F₂ are the distances to the nearest and second nearest Worley points (cell 54 px), and c is the crackle slider. The line darkens the glaze._ (primitives: crackle-network, multiply-blend)

$$\text{flower if } T(\mathbf x)\le 0.34\,(0.4+q)\ \text{ and }\ \xi\le q$$

_Where a flower may sit: q is the flowers slider and ξ a random number per grid point._ (primitives: importance-sampling, seeded-random)

**Dials:** `levels` (Vines) = 8 [3 to 14]; `smooth` (Smoothness) = 5 [1 to 10]; `leaves` (Leaves) = 70 [0 to 100]; `flowers` (Flowers) = 55 [0 to 100]; `wash` (Blue wash) = 55 [0 to 100]; `crackle` (Crackle glaze) = 50 [0 to 100]; `ground` (Glaze) = "white" (white, cream, turquoise).

**History:** Hala, a town in the Matiari district, is one of the places where blue pottery is still made in Sindh. It shares a tradition with Multan and with Persia, and Sindh has a long tradition of blue glazed tile, as seen on the Shah Jahan Mosque at Thatta (1647). Potters work in small family kilns, and the craft depends on knowledge passed from one generation to the next.

## Notes

- Hala and Multan blue pottery (`lumenary:hala-blue-pottery`) is filed here because Lumenary's model paints on a white
  glaze, and because the brief for C-017 named it as a variant of tin-glaze painting. That example was wrong as stated:
  this ware is usually described as painted on a white slip under a clear alkaline glaze, not on tin glaze. Check the
  sources when this page is written; it may belong under its own process (underglaze painting on slip).

### Batik (`lumenary:batik`, models this process)

_From: Java, Indonesia; when: Centuries old; works best on: Pictures with clear shapes and mid tones._

Batik keeps dye out of cloth. Where hot wax lies, the cloth stays the colour it was, so the maker draws first, dyes next, and adds more wax between baths, working from light to dark. The look is dotted outlines, small marks that fill the shapes and a web of fine cracks where the dye crept through broken wax.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Match your picture to a cream (the undyed cloth) and six dyes, according to the scheme: soga browns, bright coastal colours or indigo.
2. Clean the regions with a majority filter.
3. Draw the cream outlines in two zones around every boundary: a thin continuous line and a wider zone of dots, as a canting makes them.
4. Fill each dye region with its own mark: dots, dashes, crosses, rings, waves or triangles.
5. Add crackle, a web of lines where dye reached the cloth through broken wax.

**The maths:**

$$g=F_2(\mathbf x)-F_1(\mathbf x),\qquad \text{vein where } g<w=(0.7+1.2\,c)\,\mathrm{sc}$$

_Crackle: F₁ and F₂ are the distances to the nearest and second nearest seed. g is zero on the boundary between two cells._ (primitives: crackle-network)

$$q=0.8\Big(1-\frac{g}{w}\Big),\qquad C\leftarrow C+q\,(C_{\text{dark}}-C)$$

_Dye creeping in: darkest in the middle of the crack and fading to nothing at its edge. It only applies where slow noise exceeds 0.62 − 0.4c._ (primitives: crackle-network, mix-over, resist-mask)

$$\Big\lVert\operatorname{frac}\!\big(\tfrac{\mathbf x}{q}\big)-\tfrac12\Big\rVert<0.17$$

_The simplest isen-isen mark: a dot in every square of side q. The others are similar conditions on the same coordinates._ (primitives: lattice-repeat)

**Dials:** `simplify` (Simplify) = 5 [1 to 10]; `marks` (Fill marks) = 70 [0 to 100]; `size` (Mark size) = 6 [3 to 12]; `crackle` (Crackle) = 45 [0 to 100]; `scheme` (Dyes) = "soga" (soga, pesisir, indigo); `seed` (New cracks) = 3.

**History:** Wax-resist dyeing is found in several parts of the world, but Java developed it into a refined art. The courts of Yogyakarta and Surakarta kept strict patterns, such as parang, in soga brown and indigo, while coastal towns favoured brighter colours. The copper stamp (cap) made batik quicker in the nineteenth century. UNESCO listed Indonesian batik as intangible heritage in 2009, and Indonesia marks National Batik Day on 2 October.

### Ajrak (`lumenary:ajrak`, models this process)

_From: Sindh, on the Indus; when: Centuries old, a cloth still made and worn today; works best on: Pictures with a mix of light, mid and dark tones._

Ajrak is made by keeping dye out of the cloth. A resist paste goes on wherever the cotton is meant to stay pale, and the cloth is then dyed in indigo and in red from madder root. The paste holds the dye back, so the white lines of the finished shawl are the places where nothing reached the cotton. The eight-pointed star and the lattice repeat across the cloth like a map of the night sky.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Lay out one block for each square of side s = W / n, where n is the number of blocks across.
2. Inside a block, use a polar test to decide what lies under every pixel: ground, star colour or resist.
3. Draw the eight-pointed star with a star polygon formula, with a resist outline, a resist ring, a resist centre and a diamond with small resist dots at each corner.
4. Dye each block in one of three states by the picture’s tone: dark blocks have a deep ground with a red star, midtones swap the two, and light blocks have a pale ground. Size each block from 82 to 100 percent of its square by the picture.
5. Print the resist from a block shifted by up to 1.5 px, and show only the shifted copy as white, since a real print is never perfect.

**The maths:**

$$r(\theta)=\frac{R_1\,p_y}{p_y\cos t-p_x\sin t},\quad t=\text{fold}(\theta)\in\big[0,\tfrac{\pi}{n}\big],\quad \begin{aligned}p_x&=R_2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{n}-R_1\\ p_y&=R_2\sin\tfrac{\pi}{n}\end{aligned}$$

_The outline of an n-pointed star (n = 8 here, R₁ = 0.94 σ, R₂ = 0.55 σ): a straight line from a tip to the next notch, written in polar form. σ is the block size factor._ (primitives: star-rosette-motif)

$$k=\begin{cases}0&t<0.36\\ 1&0.36\le t<0.66\\ 2&t\ge0.66\end{cases},\qquad \sigma=0.82+0.18\,(1-t)$$

_The dye state k and block size σ from the tone t of the picture in that block. With probability 1 − f (f is “colour follows picture”) the state is a chequerboard (i + j) mod 2 instead._ (primitives: tone-bands, seeded-random)

$$z=\begin{cases}\text{resist}&Z(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta)=\text{resist}\\ \text{ground}&Z(\mathbf x)=\text{resist},\ Z(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta)\ne\text{resist}\\ Z(\mathbf x)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

_Misregistration: the block zone map Z is read twice, the second time shifted by δ = (1, 0.7) × 1.5 px × the hand-printed slider. White shows only where the shifted copy says so._ (primitives: misregistration, resist-mask)

**Dials:** `size` (Block size) = 48 [28 to 90]; `follow` (Colour follows picture) = 85 [0 to 100]; `dye` (Dye) = "classic" (classic, madder, indigo); `border` (End borders) = 60 [0 to 100]; `print` (Hand-printed look) = 55 [0 to 100].

**History:** Ajrak is the block-printed cloth of Sindh, pressed onto cotton by carved wooden blocks in stages. Many Sindhis point to the trefoil-patterned shawl on the Mohenjo-daro bust as a distant ancestor, a story told with affection and not proved. The shawl is given as a gift of honour to guests, worn on the shoulder, and worn on Sindhi Cultural Day, which is celebrated on the first Sunday of December. Printing families in Sindh and across the border in Kutch have kept the craft alive.

### Double Exposure (`lumenary:double-exposure`, models this process)

_From: Early photography and cinema; when: 1860s onward, a design trend in the 2010s; works best on: Portraits and profiles against a plain background._

When two pictures are recorded on one frame of film, light adds, so one picture shows through wherever the other was dark. This treatment recreates that with a window: the photograph’s tones decide where a second scene shows through, and the two are toned in the same pair of inks so they feel like one image. The second scene is drawn from scratch, with distant layers lighter than near ones for depth.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Tone the photograph into two inks, a dark and a light one, with a ramp over its lightness.
2. Compare its blurred tones with a level, using a smoothstep whose width is the softness of the window, and decide whether dark or light parts open it.
3. Draw the second scene in the same inks: mountains from layered noise, pine rows from stacked triangles, a city from three ranks of buildings, or a moon with stars and clouds.
4. Make each far layer lighter and each near one darker, with fog between them for aerial perspective.
5. Inside the window mix the scene in with a screen blend, 80 percent replacement and 20 percent film, with the photograph’s lightness modulating the scene’s brightness.

**The maths:**

$$m=\operatorname{smoothstep}\big(\lambda-\sigma,\ \lambda+\sigma,\ v\big),\qquad v=\begin{cases}L_b&\text{light opens}\\ 1-L_b&\text{dark opens}\end{cases}$$

_The window: λ is the level slider and σ = 0.02 + 0.5 × the softness slider._ (primitives: soft-threshold)

$$s'=s\cdot\operatorname{clip}\big(0.87+0.9\,D\,(L-\tfrac12),\ 0,\ 1.15\big)$$

_The scene’s brightness follows the photograph’s lightness L; D is the photo detail slider._ (primitives: multiply-blend, tone-curve)

$$C=0.8\Big[(1-0.96\,m)\,b+0.96\,m\,s'\Big]+0.2\Big[255-\frac{(255-b)\,(255-0.55\,m\,s')}{255}\Big]$$

_The base b (the toned photograph) with the scene inside the window, plus a screen blend of the two._ (primitives: mix-over, additive-blend)

**Dials:** `scene` (Second scene) = "mountains" (mountains, pines, skyline, moon); `tint` (Ink) = "blue" (blue, sepia, mono, rose, forest); `fill` (Scene shows in) = "dark" (dark, light); `level` (Where the window opens) = 38 [10 to 90]; `soft` (Softness of the window) = 28 [5 to 60]; `detail` (Photo detail kept) = 35 [0 to 100]; `seed` (New scene) = 1.

**History:** Double exposures happened by accident in the earliest photographs and were used on purpose in the 1860s by spirit photographers who printed ghostly figures over portraits. Filmmakers such as Georges Méliès used them for early special effects. The portrait-with-a-landscape version became a design trend in the 2010s, and it was widely seen in the title sequence of the TV series True Detective in 2014.

### Chronophotograph (`lumenary:chronophotograph`, models this process)

_From: Early motion study in physiology; when: Marey’s photographic gun of 1882 and fixed-plate work after it; works best on: A bright subject moving across a dark background._

Before film, nobody could say exactly what a galloping horse’s legs were doing. Marey answered by exposing one plate again and again, so that every instant of a movement lay on the same picture. It works because a bright figure on a black ground adds light to the plate wherever it goes and takes none away. Strobe photography still does the same, and the result shows time as a shape.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Turn each frame into what goes on the plate: a toned picture with the background pushed towards black.
2. Every fraction of a second, keep a copy of the current frame in a ring of past exposures.
3. Lay the exposures down from the oldest to the newest, each dimmer the older it is, always keeping the brighter value.
4. On video, optionally find what moves by comparing with the still background, and lay down only that.
5. On a still picture, slide a smaller copy across the plate on a swinging path, so there is motion to record.
6. Add a faint measuring grid and a dark vignette like a laboratory plate.

**The maths:**

$$E=\ell^{0.92}\,\operatorname{smoothstep}(a,\ a+0.4,\ \ell),\qquad w_j=\Big(1-\frac{j}{K}\Big)^{x}$$

_An exposure with its background crushed, and the weight of an exposure that is j steps old._ (primitives: tone-curve, soft-threshold, decay-trail)

$$P=\max_{j}\ \big(w_j\,E_j\big)$$

_The plate keeps the brightest value that any exposure gave, in each colour channel._ (primitives: frame-history)

$$\mathbf x(t)=\big(0.29\,W\sin 1.3t,\ \ 0.03\,H\sin(2.6t+0.8)\big)$$

_The path a still picture is swung along, so that there is motion to record._ (primitives: parametric-curve)

**Dials:** `shots` (Exposures) = 9 [3 to 16]; `gap` (Time between exposures) = 160 [40 to 600]; `fade` (Fade of the older ones) = 55 [0 to 100]; `ground` (Background) = "dark" (dark, keep, movers); `crush` (Black point) = 45 [0 to 100]; `tone` (Plate) = "silver" (silver, sepia, colour); `grid` (Measuring grid) = true.

**History:** Étienne-Jules Marey, a French physiologist, wanted to measure how bodies move. In 1882 he built a camera shaped like a gun that took twelve exposures a second on one rotating plate, and soon after he moved to a fixed plate with a single lens, dressing his subjects in black with white lines on the limbs so that their paths showed as lines. His pictures influenced the Futurists and Duchamp’s Nude Descending a Staircase (1912). Harold Edgerton’s flash photographs, from the 1930s, brought the method into modern science and the studio.

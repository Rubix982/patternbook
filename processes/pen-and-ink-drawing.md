### Parchment (`lumenary:parchment`, a style that rests on this process)

_From: Manuscript books on animal skin; when: About 200 BC to 1500; works best on: Figures and objects with clear light and shade._

Before paper reached Europe, books were written on prepared animal skin, which is tough, slightly translucent and never quite flat or even in colour. Ink bit into it and has since browned. A page was planned like a building: a ruled text block, a picture in a painted frame, and a large red initial to mark where reading begins.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Build the skin: mottling, a darker rim, rusty foxing and fine pores.
2. Shade the picture with parallel pen lines that thicken as the tone darkens.
3. Cross them with a second set in the deepest shadows, and add outlines.
4. Thin the ink unevenly and fade it in patches.
5. Frame the picture in red and gold, rule the lines, paint the capital and write the script.

**The maths:**

$$s=\operatorname{clamp}\big(0.9\,a\,(m-0.35)+\text{rim}\,(0.35+0.5a)+0.5\,a\,\text{fox}\big)$$

_Stain amount: m is the mottling noise, a the age slider. The skin colour is a blend from vellum to brown by s._ (primitives: material-ground, sheet-edge)

$$\alpha_1=1-S_{0.8w,\,0.8w+0.22}(d_1),\qquad w=\operatorname{clamp}\big(1.25\,(D-0.22)\big)\cdot h$$

_Hatching: d₁ is the distance to the nearest line centre (0 to 1), D the darkness of the picture, h the hatching slider._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

$$d_1=2\,\Big\lvert\operatorname{frac}\!\Big(\frac{y+0.35x+6\sin(0.012y+0.004x)}{p}\Big)-\tfrac12\Big\rvert$$

_Lines every p = 5.2 px, slanted and gently waved._ (primitives: tone-line-screen)

**Dials:** `age` (Age of the skin) = 55 [0 to 100]; `hatch` (Pen hatching) = 60 [0 to 100]; `lines` (Outlines) = 55 [0 to 100]; `ink` (Ink) = "gall" (gall, black, sepia, blue); `layout` (Page) = "leaf" (leaf, full); `text` (Script) = "Here begins the book of light, in which every picture is written twice, once by the sun and once by the hand."; `seed` (Another leaf) = 1.

**History:** Parchment takes its name from Pergamon in Asia Minor, where by tradition it was perfected in the second century BC. From late antiquity to the nineteenth century most European writing was done in iron gall ink, made from oak galls and iron salts, which goes on pale and darkens to brown-black. Monastic scribes developed the illuminated page, with red headings called rubrics and gold leaf, as in the Book of Kells of about 800.

### Blind Contour (`lumenary:blind-contour`, models this process)

_From: Art school drawing exercises; when: 20th century; works best on: Objects and faces with clear outlines._

In a blind contour drawing the artist looks only at the subject and never at the page, with the pen kept on the paper. The line wanders and lands near the edge but not on it, and the result is more alive than a careful tracing. This treatment finds the edges, chains them into one continuous pen line and lets it drift.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Find the picture’s edges with the Sobel gradient of a lightly blurred, stretched lightness.
2. Divide the picture into small cells and keep the strongest edge pixel in each if it passes a threshold.
3. Chain the points: start at one, jump to the nearest unused point within 2.6 cells, and repeat until none is near enough, then lift the pen and start again.
4. Drop lines shorter than four points.
5. Add drift to every point, smooth noise along the drawing plus a little jitter, and join them with quadratic curves through the midpoints so the line has no corners.

**The maths:**

$$\mathcal P=\Big\{\ \arg\max_{\text{cell}}E\ :\ \max_{\text{cell}}E>\vartheta\ \Big\},\qquad \vartheta=0.1-0.07\,D$$

_Edge points: the strongest Sobel response E in each cell, if above the threshold set by the detail slider D._ (primitives: image-gradient)

$$\mathbf q_{i+1}=\arg\min_{\mathbf q\in\mathcal P\,\setminus\,\text{used}}\lVert\mathbf q-\mathbf q_i\rVert\qquad\text{if}\quad\lVert\mathbf q_{i+1}-\mathbf q_i\rVert<2.6\,g$$

_Chaining the points into one pen line; g is the cell size._ (written for this model: specific)

$$\tilde{\mathbf q}_i=\mathbf q_i+2A\,\big(\boldsymbol\nu(0.0045\,i)-\tfrac12\big)+0.35\,A\,\big(\mathbf u-\tfrac12\big)$$

_The hand drifts: ν is smooth noise along the line, u random numbers in [0, 1], A = 14 px × the drift slider._ (primitives: smooth-noise, seeded-random)

**Dials:** `detail` (How much is drawn) = 55 [0 to 100]; `drift` (Hand drift) = 45 [0 to 100]; `weight` (Line weight) = 3 [1 to 10]; `ink` (Ink) = "black" (black, blue, red, green); `paper` (Paper) = "cream" (white, cream); `seed` (Draw again) = 1.

**History:** The exercise was popularised by the art teacher Kimon Nicolaïdes in his 1941 book The Natural Way to Draw, and later by Betty Edwards’s Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain. It trains the eye to see edges as they are rather than as we assume them to be, and it has become a common warm-up in art classes.

### Poisson Stippling (`lumenary:poisson-stippling`, models this process)

_From: Sampling theory and scientific illustration; when: 1980s computer graphics; works best on: Portraits and engravings-style subjects._

Random dots clump and leave gaps, while a grid shows its rows. The eye prefers something in between: dots that are random but never too close. This is called blue noise, and it looks smooth and natural because it has no long-range pattern. Here the minimum distance shrinks in the shadows, so the dots crowd together there and thin out in the lights.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Set the minimum distance r(x) = r_max − (r_max − r_min) h^γ, where h is the darkness under the point and γ the contrast.
2. Try random points, up to 4.5 times the area divided by r_min², at most 900,000 of them.
3. Keep a point only if its distance from every dot kept so far is at least the mean of the two radii.
4. Use a grid of cells r_max wide so only the nine nearest cells need checking.
5. Draw each dot in ink, sepia or the picture’s colour.

**The maths:**

$$r(\mathbf x)=r_{\max}-\big(r_{\max}-r_{\min}\big)\,h(\mathbf x)^{\gamma}$$

_The local minimum spacing; h is the heaviness of the picture at x._ (primitives: poisson-disc, importance-sampling, tone-curve)

$$\text{keep }\mathbf p\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert\ \ge\ \tfrac12\big(r(\mathbf p)+r(\mathbf q)\big)\quad\text{for every kept }\mathbf q$$

_The acceptance rule of the dart throwing._ (primitives: poisson-disc)

$$\rho(\mathbf x)\propto\frac{1}{r(\mathbf x)^{2}},\qquad \rho_{\text{dot}}=r_p\,k\,\big(1+0.95\,h^{2}\big)$$

_Approximate density of dots, and the drawn dot radius (k is the dot size slider)._ (primitives: importance-sampling, tone-mark-size)

**Dials:** `fine` (Finest spacing) = 3 [1.5 to 8]; `range` (Spacing in the lights) = 5 [2 to 10]; `gamma` (Contrast) = 100 [40 to 200]; `dot` (Dot size) = 38 [15 to 70]; `colour` (Ink) = "ink" (ink, sepia, picture); `paper` (Paper) = "cream" (cream, night); `seed` (Scatter again) = 1.

**History:** Stippling is an old technique in pen and ink illustration, used for botany and anatomy. Robert Cook introduced stochastic sampling to computer graphics in 1986, and Don Mitchell and others developed blue noise sampling in the early 1990s. Robert Bridson published a fast algorithm in 2007. The psychologist John Yellott observed in 1983 that the receptor cells in a monkey’s eye are arranged in a similar pattern.

## patternbook's model: `hatch`

_The theory of patternbook's own `hatch` treatment (`treatments/treatments.js`), "Pen hatching", moved here from `treatments/theory/hatch.md` on 2026-11-22 so it sits with the process it models._

**For:** photographs on a page in a pen-and-ink style.
**Comes from:** engraving and pen illustration, where tone is built only from lines.
**Works best on:** objects with clear form and light from one side.

### Why it looks this way

A pen makes a line of one colour, so shade has to come from how many lines there are and how
close together. Illustrators add a direction for each step darker: one set of parallel strokes
for light shade, a second crossing it for mid-tones, more for shadows. A hand-drawn stroke
isn't straight or even. It drifts slightly and swells and thins with pressure. Outlines are
drawn where one surface meets another.

### How it works

1. Brightness L, blurred a little; darkness t = 1 − contrast(L).
2. Four layers of parallel lines at 45°, −45°, 8° and 98°, switched on at darkness 0.22, 0.42,
   0.62 and 0.80.
3. Each line drifts sideways with smooth noise along its length and changes thickness with
   "pressure".
4. Add an outline where the Sobel edge strength passes `outline`.

### The maths

For layer k at angle φ, measure the position across the lines, a = x cos φ + y sin φ, and
along them, b = −x sin φ + y cos φ. With spacing p:

    q = (a + w) / p,      w = wobble · (2n(b / λ, a / 3p) − 1)
    dist = |q − round(q)| · p
    half-width = p · (0.1 + 0.22 · (t − t_k) / (1 − t_k)) · (0.55 + 0.45 · pressure)
    ink_k = smoothstep(hw + 0.6 px, hw − 0.6 px, dist) · smoothstep(t_k − 0.04, t_k + 0.06, t)

Layers combine like ink: paper = Π(1 − ink_k), and the outline multiplies in last.

### Dials

`spacing` (4 px), `contrast` (0.2), `outline` (0.35), `wobble` (0.8 px), `wobbleScale` (14 px),
`fibre`, `ink`, `paper`.

### When it fights the content

- **Flat graphics.** With no tonal shading there is nothing to hatch; use `press`.
- **Busy photos.** Hatching on a cluttered background becomes noise. Crop to the subject first.

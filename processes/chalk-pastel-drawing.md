### Pastel Chalk (`lumenary:pastel-chalk`, models this process)

_From: European portraiture and studio drawing; when: 16th century onward, popular in the 18th and 19th centuries; works best on: Portraits, skies and flowers._

Pastel is used on toned paper. The paper’s own colour supplies the shadows and middle tones, and the chalk goes on mostly for the lights, which is why pastel pictures glow. This treatment lays short wide strokes of lifted colour, only where the picture is light, and breaks each stroke on the grain of the paper.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Paint a layer of short, wide strokes in two passes, the second half the size, along a main direction with a spread of about 0.35 radians.
2. Give each stroke the colour of the blurred picture with saturation raised by 12 percent and lightness set to 0.9 l plus 0.1, so chalk comes out brighter and softer.
3. Decide how much chalk stays from how light the picture is there, times the pressure, times a slow noise, so shadows are left to the paper.
4. Break each stroke with the paper tooth, a mix of smooth and white noise, which spatters it into dots.
5. Add a few specks of loose dust and mix paper and chalk by the final coverage.

**The maths:**

$$\text{amt}=\operatorname{clip}\!\Big[(0.15+1.05\,L_s)\,P\,(0.8+0.4\,n)\Big],\qquad P=0.35+0.9\,\text{pressure}$$

_Chalk offered to the paper: L_s is the blurred lightness, n slow noise._ (primitives: smooth-noise)

$$m=\alpha\ \operatorname{smoothstep}\!\Big(1.1\,\tau q-0.12(1-q)-0.05,\ \ 1.1\,\tau q+0.2+0.1(1-q),\ \ \text{amt}\Big)$$

_Coverage: α is the stroke layer’s own alpha, τ the paper tooth noise, q the tooth slider._ (primitives: tooth-threshold)

$$C=C_{\text{paper}}\,(1-m)+C_{\text{chalk}}\,m$$

_Chalk over toned paper._ (primitives: mix-over)

**Dials:** `size` (Stroke size) = 40 [0 to 100]; `pressure` (Pressure) = 70 [10 to 100]; `tooth` (Paper tooth) = 55 [0 to 100]; `angle` (Direction of strokes) = 55 [0 to 180]; `paper` (Paper) = "slate" (slate, navy, kraft, black); `seed` (Redraw) = 1.

**History:** Artists drew with dry coloured chalks as early as the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and pastel portraiture flourished in the eighteenth century with painters such as Rosalba Carriera and Jean-Étienne Liotard. Edgar Degas used pastel boldly in the 1870s to 1890s. Its soft, powdery finish is still prized for portraits and skies.

### Chalkboard (`lumenary:chalkboard`, models this process)

_From: School and café blackboards; when: About 1801 onward; works best on: Pictures with bright subjects and clear outlines._

Chalk is the one common drawing tool that works by adding light to a dark ground. It is also dry and crumbly, so it only sticks to the high points of the board and every stroke is a scatter of grains. And a board has a memory: yesterday’s work is never fully erased, only spread about. Those three facts make the look.

**How it is made, as the model does it:**

1. Paint the board: uneven slate with broad eraser sweeps of old chalk.
2. Make a tooth map of the board’s high points.
3. Draw the outlines in chalk, broken by the tooth.
4. Rub chalk into the bright areas with slanted side-of-the-stick strokes.
5. With coloured sticks, choose the stick nearest in hue to the picture.
6. Add the wooden frame, the tray, chalk and an eraser.

**The maths:**

$$C_{\text{board}}=C_0\,(0.8+0.34\,m)+(C_{\text{chalk}}-C_0)\,(0.05+0.2\,\text{wipe})\cdot\text{dust}$$

_The slate: m is mottling noise, wipe the eraser sweeps, dust the smears slider._ (primitives: material-ground)

$$q=S_{0.42,\,0.95}(L)\cdot s\cdot(0.55+0.6\,\text{stroke}),\qquad \alpha=0.82\,S_{0.95-0.9q,\ 1.25-0.75q}(\text{tooth}+0.35)$$

_Side-of-chalk shading: q is how much chalk is wanted, s the shading slider._ (primitives: soft-threshold, tooth-threshold)

$$\alpha_{\text{line}}=S_{\theta,\,2.2\theta}(E)\cdot S_{0.18,\,0.5}(\text{tooth}+0.25),\qquad\theta=\operatorname{lerp}(0.16,\ 0.05,\ \text{lines})$$

_Outlines, broken by the tooth of the board._ (primitives: edge-outline, tooth-threshold)

**Dials:** `lines` (Outlines) = 60 [0 to 100]; `shade` (Side-of-chalk shading) = 60 [0 to 100]; `dust` (Eraser smears) = 50 [0 to 100]; `chalk` (Chalk) = "white" (white, colour); `board` (Board) = "green" (green, black, blue); `frame` (Frame and tray) = true; `seed` (Wipe and redraw) = 1.

**History:** James Pillans, a headmaster in Edinburgh, is credited with hanging a large slate on the classroom wall around 1801 so that a whole class could see one lesson. By the mid nineteenth century blackboards were in almost every school. Green boards of enamelled steel arrived in the twentieth century because they were lighter and easier on the eyes. School “chalk” has long been made of gypsum, not true chalk.

How to replace a bicycle chain

A worn chain wears out your cassette and chainrings with it. Swapping it is one of the cheapest, most useful jobs you can do yourself. Here is the whole process, start to finish.

Time: 20–40 minDifficulty: Easy–moderateCost: chain ~$15–60

First, do you actually need a new chain?

Chains don't really "stretch" — the pins and rollers wear, so the spacing between links grows. The easiest check is a chain-checker tool. Drop it into the chain and see whether the tip falls all the way in.

No checker? Use a ruler. On a new chain, 12 full links measure exactly 12 inches pin-to-pin (304.8 mm).

12 links measureWhat it means
12 in exactlyLike new.
12 1/16 in (≈0.5%)Replace now, especially on 11–12 speed. Cassette is likely still fine.
12 1/8 in (≈1%)Overdue. The cassette and maybe chainrings are probably worn too; a new chain may skip on old cogs.

What you'll need

  • New chain requiredMatched to your speed count (e.g. 11-speed). Check the number of cogs on your rear cassette.
  • Chain tool requiredPushes pins out to break and shorten the chain. Many multitools have one.
  • Master-link pliers helpfulOpens and closes quick links. You can close one by pedalling without the tool.
  • Rags and degreaser helpfulGood moment to clean the cassette and chainrings.
  • Chain lube requiredNew chains come with factory grease, but a light lube afterward helps.
  • Gloves helpfulChains are filthy.
Match the speed. An 8-speed chain on an 11-speed drivetrain will not shift properly — the chain width differs. Brand mixing (Shimano, SRAM, KMC) is usually fine if the speed count matches.

Know the parts of a chain

A chain alternates between inner links (narrow) and outer links (wide). You can only join an inner link to an outer link, or two inner ends with a master link. Get this right and the rest follows.

Step by step

0 of 7 steps done

1

Shift to the smallest cogs

Shift onto the small chainring at the front and the smallest cog at the back. This puts the least tension on the chain and makes it easy to handle. If you can, put the bike in a stand or hang it so the rear wheel spins.

2

Remove the old chain

If there's a master link, squeeze it with master-link pliers (or open it by pushing the two plates toward each other). Otherwise, use the chain tool: seat any outer link in the tool, turn the handle until the pin pushes all the way out, and pull the chain apart.

3

Size the new chain

New chains come too long. Two common ways to find the right length:

  • Copy the old chain. Lay the old and new chains side by side, matching link for link (not by length — the old one is worn and longer). Simple and reliable if the old chain was the right length.
  • Big-big plus one. Wrap the new chain around the largest chainring and largest cog, bypassing the rear derailleur. Find where the ends would join, then add one inch (one inner + one outer link). If using a master link, the master link counts as part of that inch.
Full-suspension bikes: size the chain with the rear shock deflated or removed and the suspension fully compressed, since chain length changes through the travel. Many 12-speed systems (SRAM Eagle, Shimano 12s) also publish their own sizing method — follow the manufacturer if it differs.
4

Cut the new chain to length

Mark the link, then use the chain tool to push out a pin. For a master-link chain, cut so that both ends are inner links (narrow ends). For a Shimano connecting-pin chain, leave one inner end and one outer end. Measure twice; you can't add links back without another master link.

5

Route the chain through the bike

Thread the chain over the front chainring and over the smallest rear cog, then down through the rear derailleur: in front of the upper jockey wheel, behind the tab between the wheels, and around the lower jockey wheel. Check it isn't passing outside the derailleur cage tabs — this is the most common mistake.

Direction matters on some chains. Shimano HG-X and many SRAM chains have a logo side or asymmetric plates. Logos usually face out (away from the bike). Check the chain's instructions.
6

Join the ends

Take the chain off the front chainring and let it rest on the frame — the slack makes joining much easier.

  • Master link: insert one half into each end from opposite sides, hook the pins into the slots, put the chain back on the chainring, then hold the rear brake and press firmly on a pedal. You'll hear a click as it locks. Master-link pliers do the same job.
  • Shimano connecting pin: push the pointed guide end into the link from the back, then drive it through with the chain tool until it sits flush with the plates like its neighbours. Snap off the guide with pliers.

Most master links for 11- and 12-speed are single use. Don't reuse one.

7

Check for stiff links and test

Backpedal slowly and watch the chain run through the derailleur. A link that jumps or kinks at the jockey wheels is stiff — usually the one you just joined. Flex it side to side with your hands until it moves freely. Then shift through every gear, front and back, and listen for skipping.

After the swap

  • Skipping under load on a brand-new chain almost always means the cassette (or a favourite cog) is worn. It will need replacing too.
  • Lube lightly. One drop per roller, spin the cranks, wipe off the excess. The factory grease is a fine base.
  • Check wear every ~1,000 miles (1,500 km). Changing chains at 0.5% usually lets one cassette last two or three chains.
  • Carry a spare master link on rides. It turns a broken chain into a five-minute roadside fix.
